### type-001 · Reading comes before expression
- **Statement:** Type exists so that people read the words and take in their meaning; expression and looks come second, so never make text hard to read. It should invite the reader in, reveal the content, make the structure clear, link the text to the other elements and bring the reader into an energetic calm that is ideal for reading.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BOULTON-090 (page 71), BRINGHURST-032 (page 26)
- **Also in (not read):** BRINGHURST-016, CABARGA-114, CABARGA-120

### type-003 · The setting of the text matters more than the font
- **Statement:** Most typographic decisions concern the space around letters and around blocks of text (line length, spacing), not the choice of typeface; the font and its colour are a small part of the work. ### type-008 · Name the feelings first, then compare candidates on the real name
- **Statement:** First define the feelings the site should evoke in its audience, then judge each candidate font against them, since fonts are not good or bad in themselves, only fitting or unfitting for the purpose: narrow the field to a few good candidates and refine until one works well; a friend or co-worker can be asked "which one makes you feel more [the intended adjective]?". In the author's example a business name ("Joe's Restaurant") is set in 20 fonts at the same size and colour, each labelled, so that only the typeface changes.

### type-009 · A good face first, fit for its use second, fit for the subject third
- **Statement:** The best face for a book (the example is a book on bicycle racing) is, in this order: first simply a good face; second a good face for books, one that does not tire the reader over long reading; only third one suited to the subject, and even then without superfluous ornament or fancy dress. A face whose letters picture the subject (spokes in the O, a racing saddle for the A) does not serve the text better.

- **Use:** look

### type-010 · Columns for continuous reading are taller than they are wide

### type-011 · Mind a face's period and nation against the text
- **Statement:** Faces carry their period, and some a national character, and that can clash with the text: books on modern Italy or 17th-century France set in Baskerville and Caslon, made in 18th-century England; a Canadian or Mexican constitution set in a face by Goudy, the most American of American type designers. Avoiding such clashes is only the minimum: the typographer sets each text in a face and form that truly belong to it, and a face that truly suits a historical text will likely carry its own clear historical content.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-228 (page 115), BRINGHURST-235 (page 119), BRINGHURST-229 (page 115)

### type-012 · Prefer the long-proven text faces to fashionable ones

### type-014 · Judge a typeface in words, not in single letters

### type-017 · A famous name does not vouch for a font: check the version
- **Statement:** Digital versions of the same classic face differ widely: some still lack essential parts such as old-style figures, some makers sell cut-down, distorted or pirated versions, and two revivals of one original can differ greatly through interpretation. ### type-018 · Expect to adjust a font's spacing and kerning for the real text

### type-022 · A large x-height reads better at small sizes
- **Statement:** The height of the lowercase letters (x-height) affects legibility, most of all at small sizes: a face with a large x-height stays legible small and uses space efficiently, and one author advises larger x-heights for serif body text on the web. ### type-024 · In print, delicate faces fail when small at low resolution
- **Statement:** In print, fine, delicate faces (Bembo, Centaur, Spectrum, Palatino; Linotype Didot and almost any Bodoni) turn to digital mud when set small and output at low resolution (8 pt on a 300 dpi laser printer), while faces with blunt heavy serifs, open counters and soft modelling (Amasis, Caecilia, Lucida, Stone, Utopia) survive it. A plain look does not prove a face is robust: Optima, a seemingly simple sans serif, renders well only at the highest resolutions. On screen the same once held: serif type at body sizes came out blurry on the poor screens of earlier computers, and NN/g finds that guideline no longer needed on today's high-quality screens.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-214 (page 110), BRINGHURST-216 (page 110-111, the notes cite 110)
- **Also in (not read):** BRINGHURST-215, BRINGHURST-401, BRINGHURST-407
- **Asked of the source:** whether fine faces still break down at text sizes on current screens; the sources speak of 300 dpi printers.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/serif-vs-sans-serif-fonts-hd-screens/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate type E26

### type-026 · Use the cut drawn for the size
- **Statement:** Type for small sizes has heavier strokes, looser spacing and often a larger x-height; type for large sizes is lighter, tighter and finer. Where a family offers optical sizes, use each at its size (display styles from about 24 pt, text styles about 9 to 14 pt, caption styles about 6 to 8 pt): a text-size design simply enlarged to poster size gets features too heavy, and a display style set small looks spindly and weak. On the web, optical sizes come as the optical-size axis of a variable font, which the browser applies from the font size by default where the font has the axis.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look

### type-027 · A word space of about a quarter em
- **Statement:** For a normal text face at text size the typical word space is a quarter em, roughly the width of a letter t. Flush-left text keeps one fixed word space; in justified text it varies, with a fifth of an em as a reasonable minimum, a quarter as the target and half an em as the maximum (better a third). Wide letter spacing, bold type, loosely spaced faces and small text need more (around a third; half an em or more for widely letterspaced capitals), and larger sizes can take less.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-036 (page 28), BRINGHURST-038 (page 29)
- **Also in (not read):** BRINGHURST-035, BRINGHURST-460

### type-029 · Optical sameness, not measured sameness
- **Statement:** Round letters hang slightly below the baseline and pointed ones pierce it, because otherwise they would look smaller than flat-bottomed letters; vertical and curved strokes are drawn heavier to look equal to horizontal and straight ones. Alignment by eye has to allow for this: what must match is the look, not the measurement.

- **Use:** look, check

### type-031 · For body text in sans serif, pick a humanist one
- **Statement:** A sans serif fit for real text has the humanist features: open aperture, uneven strokes, a true italic with a modest slope, old-style figures and small capitals. Humanist sans serifs such as Frutiger and Meta soften geometric construction and are easier to read for it; Caspari, which has all the features, is named one of the first sans serifs designed specifically for setting text.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-417 (page 278), KIRSANOV-170 (page 63)
- **Also in (not read):** KIRSANOV-168, KIRSANOV-171

### type-032 · How much leading depends on the face and the content
- **Statement:** Dark faces need more leading than light ones, faces with a large x-height more than those with a small one, faces with a vertical axis (Bauer Bodoni) more than those with a slanted axis (Bembo), and sans serifs often more leading, or a shorter line, than serif faces. Extra leading is also welcome where the text is full of superscripts, subscripts, formulas or capitals, so German, with its many capitals, ideally takes slightly more than the same text in Latin or French.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-077 (page 41-42), BRINGHURST-078 (page 42)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate spacing E34

### type-038 · Monospaced faces only where characters must line up
- **Statement:** Use fixed-width fonts where characters must line up from row to row, such as code or data shown as text, and style such blocks so they stand out from running text. Elsewhere avoid them: equal widths stretch narrow letters, squeeze wide ones and make the text colour uneven.

### type-039 · Decorative faces: headings only, and sparingly

Gillenwater sets a casual cursive face for the headings only and keeps it off the body copy, because at small sizes such a face does not look good and is less legible.
