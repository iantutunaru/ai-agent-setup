### persuasion-001 · Use surprise only where it carries the message
- **Statement:** Surprise leaves a stronger memory than an anticipated event, breaks the user's habitual pattern and focuses attention. A surprise that has nothing to do with the message is remembered while the message is lost, so surprise pays only when it reinforces the core message.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** HEATH-030 (page s39), WALTER-044 (page 57)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E76

### persuasion-003 · Attractive design is perceived to work better
- **Statement:** Attractive design puts people in a good mood, which helps them solve problems they meet in the interface, so attractive things are perceived to work better. People forgive a product's shortcomings, follow its lead and recommend it when it rewards them with positive emotion.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** WALTER-023 (page 35), WALTER-012 (page 23)

### persuasion-006 · The first impression decides
- **Statement:** A first impression decides a lot: voters appear to choose largely with the automatic system, so a candidate who does not make a good impression at once, or who relies on complex arguments and statistics, struggles. ### persuasion-009 · An ad links to a page that continues its message, not to the home page
- **Statement:** Link an ad to a page that continues its message, not to the home page: research on web advertising found that about 20 to 30% of users who click a banner press Back at once when they see they have landed on a corporate page. Users want the information at once, within five clicks, and have little reason to fight the site's navigation. The same holds for a URL printed in an advert or on a product: an ad that promises "10 reasons to buy DVD" and lands on a home page without them, or an ad URL that opens a page unrelated to the advertised product, makes users give up, judge the site worthless and distrust the company's ads.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, check, text
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-099 (page 97), NIELSEN-102 (page 99), NIELSEN-410 (page 302)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** web advertising research, not named on the page
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/ (read 2026-11-21)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E97, joined by trust E25

### persuasion-010 · An ad runs in three acts: hook, message, close
- **Statement:** An ad leads the reader from an opener that catches attention, through the core message giving the reason to choose the product, to a final call (a phone number in print, "click here" on a banner); the close appeals to feeling again and reveals the advertiser, announces its address or simply calls for the click. In a static banner the acts run left to right, so the close sits at the right end; in an animated one they follow in time, and in the author's example, an IBM banner, the hook takes seven frames and the message and the close one frame each.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-466 (page 140, 142, the notes cite 140), KIRSANOV-264 (page 82)
- **Also in (not read):** KIRSANOV-560, KIRSANOV-561, KIRSANOV-473
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E98

### persuasion-011 · Close an ad with a short imperative call
- **Statement:** Adding "click here" is said to raise a banner's click rate by 10 to 15 percent, because the familiar formula closes the story and pushes the viewer from watching to acting. Short advertising copy may drop formal grammar; short imperative action words such as "click", "try", "go" do the work.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-467 (page 140), KIRSANOV-468 (page 141)
- **Needs source:** that an explicit "click here" still raises the click rate of an ad; the figure is from 1999 and its research is not named.
- **Current source:** none found (searched 2026-11-21)

### persuasion-013 · Movement draws the eye
- **Statement:** The survival-driven part of the brain keeps scanning for change and judges each change by danger, food or sex. Even slight movement, rotation or blinking in a banner draws the eye and is said to raise clicks; movement dominates banner design, often at the cost of its balance. Today, moving or blinking content that starts by itself, lasts more than five seconds and runs beside other content needs a way to pause, stop or hide it (WCAG 2.2.2), and Google Ads requires an ad's animation to stop after 30 seconds.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-465 (page 140), NEURO-047 (page 79)
- **Needs source:** that movement in an ad still raises clicks today
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/ (read 2026-11-21)
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html (read 2026-11-21)
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722096?hl=en (read 2026-11-21)
- **Conflict:** NIELSEN-101 (in persuasion-061) says banners that only shout "look at me" with animation carry no reason to click, and KIRSANOV-331 (in layout-033) that users filter out gaudy rectangles of known size.
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E102

### persuasion-016 · Run a campaign as a series of ads with different openings
- **Statement:** Run a campaign as a series of related ads that differ in their opening (one a cartoon, another a text question) to reach different people, and reveal that they belong to one series only at the end, so as not to lose the attention of those who have seen the others. Vary their file sizes too, so that light ones reach people on slow connections and heavy ones impress with animation those for whom loading time does not matter. In IBM's ConferenceTracker series (about 15, 10 and 3.5 kB) the two lighter banners end on the same slogan beside the same cartoon face.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-481 (page 145), KIRSANOV-563 (page 145)
- **Needs source:** whether varying an ad's file size for slow connections still matters, and at what sizes; the figures are of 1999.
- **Current source:** none found (searched 2026-11-21)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E107

### persuasion-025 · A mascot or a face-like interface wins people over

### persuasion-028 · With many options people stop weighing everything
- **Statement:** With few, well-understood options people weigh every attribute and trade them off; with many options they switch to simplifying strategies, which can be worrying for them. The more options and attributes there are, the more people simplify and the more the structure the designer gives the choice steers the outcome: with three items the order hardly matters, with many it does.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** THALER-115 (page 91-92), THALER-118 (page 92)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate search-and-filters E61

### persuasion-029 · Do not mix ready-made bundles in among single options
- **Statement:** Ready-made bundles make a good set of defaults, but when they are mixed in among single options most people do not know how to use them. When three all-in-one "lifestyle" funds were mixed into a list with six single funds, members who picked the conservative one put only 31% in it and spread the rest, ending up 77% in shares without knowing.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** THALER-148 (page 117)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate search-and-filters E67

### persuasion-030 · The make-up of the menu sways what people end up with
- **Statement:** Faced with n options, people tend to spread evenly across them (the 1/n rule), even experts such as economics professors, so the mix of options offered can strongly sway what people end up with. University staff split money evenly between whatever two funds were offered, so their share in shares depended on which pair they saw; across 170 companies, the more share funds a plan offered, the more its members put into shares.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** THALER-147 (page 116-117), THALER-145 (page 115-116, the notes cite 116)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** THALER-147: university employees choosing between two funds in three versions; Benartzi and Thaler, plans in 170 companies.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v91y2001i1p79-98.html (read 2026-11-21)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate search-and-filters E68

### persuasion-034 · A first choice is almost never revisited
- **Statement:** Once made, choices are almost never revisited: in the Swedish pension scheme 1.7%, 2.7% and 3.1% of members changed anything in each of the first three years, so the conditions at the moment of first choice probably shape outcomes for years. Those enrolled by default seldom make the follow-up choices themselves: in New Zealand's KiwiSaver two thirds of people who joined on their own picked their investment, against 8% of those enrolled automatically.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** THALER-169 (page 140), THALER-142 (page 110)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** THALER-169: Swedish pension scheme, share of members making any change, first three years; THALER-142: KiwiSaver, New Zealand, from 2007.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/when-nudging-forever-case-sweden (read 2026-11-21)
- **Conflict:** Cronqvist, Thaler and Yu 2018, as Chicago Booth Review reports it: over 16 years the members who actively chose their first portfolio made a median of one trade a year (https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/when-nudging-forever-case-sweden, read 2026-11-21)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate forms E104

### persuasion-035 · No design is neutral: every choice has a default and a description
- **Statement:** Every point in a choice system must have a rule for what happens if the person does nothing, so the designer always chooses a default, whether on purpose or not. There may be no neutral way to present it either: even factual words about a default ("put together by experts", "low fees") may count as encouragement, and how the designer describes it decides how many people take it.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** THALER-098 (page 84), THALER-164 (page 135-136)
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate forms E105

### persuasion-036 · Set a default from what is known about the person, never at random
- **Statement:** Use what is already known about a person to set their default: last year's prescriptions predict next year's drugs well, surely better than a plan picked at random. In Maine, matching drug plans to each person's last three months of prescriptions, with opt-out, put every eligible person on a plan covering 90 to 100% of their drugs. Assigning people to a plan at random left many on plans that did not cover their drugs, and over 22% of the poorest members in one survey stopped taking drugs for a time or for good because of problems with the new plan.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** THALER-177 (page 155-156), THALER-176 (page 150-151)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** THALER-177: Maine's intelligent enrolment of dual-eligible members, and the US Government Accountability Office report on random assignment; THALER-176: survey of people eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid.
- **Read:** 2026-11-21, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate forms E110

### persuasion-039 · The framing of a fact changes the decision
- **Statement:** How a fact is framed changes decisions even when the meaning is identical: "90 of 100 patients are alive after five years" leads people, doctors included, to choose an operation more often than "10 of 100 have died". Speaking to a family as "the donor's relatives" makes it easier for them to accept the donation. People accept a question as it is put and rarely reframe it, so wording is used with care.

### persuasion-043 · A comparison with the average backfires for those doing better
- **Statement:** Of almost 300 households told the neighbourhood's average electricity use, heavy users cut their use but light users increased theirs. A happy face added for below-average users stopped them raising consumption, and a sad face for above-average users made them cut more than the figures alone did. The authors' advice: never let people know they are doing better than the norm; in the study an approving face removed the backfire.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, text
