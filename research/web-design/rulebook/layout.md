### layout-001 · Settle the structure in wireframes before any styling
- **Statement:** Before any visual design, block the page out as plain rectangles on a grid: place the biggest block first (usually the content), then navigation, identity and footer, and try several arrangements before choosing. With the structure settled first, a later dislike of the images, colours or fonts leaves everything in the right place and changes stay quick.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** GILLENWATER-035 (page 63), BEAIRD-028 (page 28)
- **Also in (not read):** GENTRY-009, GENTRY-117, GENTRY-055, BEAIRD-032
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E01

### layout-002 · Start a page from its standard blocks
- **Statement:** Most sites are built from the same blocks: a container (fluid or fixed width), the logo or identity, navigation, content, a footer, and whitespace, which counts as a block of its own. A first sketch can place these before any real content exists.

### layout-003 · What the footer holds
- **Statement:** The footer holds copyright, contact and legal details and a few links to the main sections, and by closing off the content it tells people they have reached the bottom of the page. In the markup, a large block of links at the foot ("fat footer") is page-wide navigation placed outside the footer element proper, which keeps the privacy, terms and accessibility information.

- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-024 (page 25), LAWSON-019 (page 64)

### layout-004 · Navigation column on the left, on the right, or none
- **Statement:** Whether the navigation column goes left or right depends on how much and what kind of content there is. Content on the left with navigation, adverts and secondary content on the right is now the more widespread arrangement, especially common on news sites, social networks and sites with large navigation; on deep pages a right-hand secondary menu stays out of the way of visitors looking for something specific. For a simple site with no secondary navigation, consider a narrow layout with no columns; the source adds that visitors increasingly look for the content on the left.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-055 (page 44), BEAIRD-056 (page 44)
- **Also in (not read):** BEAIRD-053, BEAIRD-266
- **Conflict:** KIRSANOV-272 (in layout-035) holds that a wider left margin holding the navigation is the more natural arrangement than a heavier right side.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E05

### layout-005 · A narrow left column is the safe standard
- **Statement:** A narrow left column (a third of the width or less) beside a wide content column is a safe standard for most projects, but such sites tend to look alike, so vary it where possible. In the example the left column is about a sixth of the width and carries the logo, the section links and the small footer links, beside one large photograph.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-054 (page 43), BEAIRD-264 (page 43)
- **Asked of the source:** that a narrow left navigation column is still a standard desktop arrangement, since the example is a site of 2014.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/vertical-nav/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Conflict:** Nielsen Norman Group (Vertical navigation, 2021): left vertical navigation suits sites with many top-level sections and translates naturally to phones, but takes space from the content and is less common on contemporary websites than a horizontal bar

### layout-007 · Put things where web conventions put them

### layout-008 · Use the conventions of the site's own kind
- **Statement:** Each kind of site (shops, colleges, blogs, restaurants and so on) has its own conventions because such sites solve the same problems; a convention spreads until it needs no explanation. Use the conventions of the site's kind unless you know you have a better idea.

### layout-009 · Every page recognisable as the same site
- **Statement:** Repeat the identity and navigation blocks and keep to one small colour palette on every page, even when the home page layout differs from the inner pages. On an intranet, which staff use daily to find information, the same placement of information and links on every page lets them work much more efficiently.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check

### layout-010 · An ad is clicked most on pages with few links, and on the home page
- **Statement:** Fewer links on a page send a larger share of visitors through each, and more text and links keep people longer: the best pages for a banner ad are information-rich, mostly text, with few links and no longer than two or three screens, since on longer pages the ad scrolls out of sight. Banner ads draw slightly more clicks on the home page than on inner pages, because new arrivals are not yet engaged with the site and are easily distracted.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-459 (page 139), KIRSANOV-461 (page 139)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate persuasion E105

### layout-014 · News and offers take limited space on the home page

### layout-015 · Every item added to the home page has a cost
- **Statement:** Decision time rises with the number of choices (Hick's Law, named as a principle with no study details), so every item added to a home page makes behaviour less predictable, and every extra button means fewer users quickly find what they need. Leave off technology badges and resist requests to squeeze more onto the page.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** WALTER-021 (page 32), NIELSEN-115 (page 106)
- **Also in (not read):** KRUG-080
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E20

### layout-016 · The home page as a cover
- **Statement:** A home page is more like a magazine cover than a contents page: many visitors go no further, so even a one-page visit should leave a pleasant memory. Corporate home pages usually centre on one large, carefully chosen photograph or drawing that shows the firm's work, its main product or its top news. The home page's style matches the rest of the site.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-305 (page 92), KIRSANOV-306 (page 92)
- **Also in (not read):** GENTRY-032, GENTRY-052, GENTRY-141
- **Conflict:** NIELSEN-627 and NIELSEN-011 (in layout-017) say a home page is not a magazine cover and should look like an interface, not a full-screen picture.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E21

### layout-017 · The home page as an interface, not a showcase
- **Statement:** A magazine cover must stand out on a crowded stand and say "buy me", but a web visitor has already arrived, so the home page should persuade them to do something on the site and look like an interface, not a full-screen picture. Design pages for users of average experience, not lavish showcase pages that impress inside the company and waste the visitor's time.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-627 (page 482), NIELSEN-011 (page 24)
- **Conflict:** KIRSANOV-305, KIRSANOV-306 and GENTRY-052 (in layout-016) treat the home page as a cover built around one large picture.

### layout-028 · A page works at a glance, and its cues are stronger than feels needed
- **Statement:** People spend far less time on each page than designers imagine, so a page must do most of its work at a glance. Users in a hurry routinely miss subtle visual cues: a cue the designer feels sticks out like a sore thumb probably needs to be twice as prominent.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-013 (page s22), KRUG-071 (page s57)

### layout-029 · Show rank by prominence
- **Statement:** Rank the elements of a page by importance and show it: the more important an item, the more prominent it is (larger, bolder, a distinctive colour, more white space, higher on the page). Related items look related (grouped, same style, same area) and nesting shows what is part of what. Making everything big, colourful and loud removes the ranking, because if everything is important nothing is.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-026 (page s30), GENTRY-074 (page 96)
- **Also in (not read):** KIRSANOV-073, BEAIRD-051
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E30

### layout-030 · A heading spans only what belongs to it
- **Statement:** The width of a heading shows what belongs together and the size of its type shows rank. - **Use:** look, check

### layout-031 · Few focal points, in an obvious order
- **Statement:** Limit the number of elements that stand out; if there are several they must form an obvious hierarchy, so that at no level do several compete for attention at once. Visual noise comes in three kinds: shouting (everything bidding for attention), disorganisation (elements not aligned to a grid) and clutter (too much on the page); shouting usually means nobody decided what matters most.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-236 (page 75), KRUG-031 (page s33)
- **Also in (not read):** WALTER-020, KRUG-027, GENTRY-049
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E32

### layout-032 · Position alone can carry a hierarchy
- **Statement:** With one type size and flush-left alignment only, placing the parts of a text in different modules of a grid builds a hierarchy by position alone (title, introduction and entries in the exercise): for example each term in its own narrow column to the left of its definition, level with the definition's first line. Weight, size and alignment are variables added afterwards.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** LUPTON-150 (page 202), LUPTON-213 (page 203)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E33

### layout-033 · Split the page into clearly defined areas
- **Statement:** Split pages into clearly defined areas so users can quickly tell which to focus on and which to ignore. One source cites eye-tracking studies it does not name: users decide in their first glances which parts may be useful and then rarely look at the rest, and areas that look like adverts are ignored entirely (banner blindness); users have long learned to filter out rectangles of known size and typical gaudiness as unrelated to, or hostile to, the page content.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-028 (page s31), KIRSANOV-331 (page 99)
- **Conflict:** KIRSANOV-465 (persuasion, "Movement draws the eye") says even slight movement or blinking in a banner draws the eye and raises clicks.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E34, joined by persuasion E103

### layout-034 · People look first at the centre and the top left
- **Statement:** The centre of a composition is where people look first and the strongest spot for emphasis; on the web the top-left corner also draws much attention from readers of left-to-right languages. Most users look straight at the middle of a home page rather than its top: in the book's example (a DVD rental site) the middle made it look like a film review site, the most prominent element was a coupon box few users need, and the search box and the main offer were under-shown.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-044 (page 38-39), NIELSEN-285 (page 211)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E35

### layout-035 · The eye moves left to right and top to bottom
- **Statement:** Trained by reading, the eye moves left to right along horizontals and top to bottom along verticals; with no strong dynamic elements, what helps this movement pleases and what hinders it irritates. So a wider left margin (often holding the navigation) is more natural than a heavier right side, and a heading above the text lets the eye fall from the visually rich top to the information-rich text below.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-272 (page 83)
- **Conflict:** BEAIRD-056 (in layout-004) reports that visitors increasingly look for the content on the left and offers the navigation column on the right as an equal choice.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E36

### layout-036 · The layout mirrors for right-to-left languages
- **Statement:** The top-left corner draws attention for readers of left-to-right languages; languages such as Hebrew and Arabic are read right to left. In grid alignment use the writing-direction values start and end, not left and right: start and end follow the writing direction, while left and right are absolute and do not change with the writing system.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-045 (page 39), ROBBINS-268 (page 571)
- **Also in (not read):** ROBBINS-228
- **Asked of the source:** that current browsers flip rows and start and end alignment by the page's writing direction (MDN).
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/CSS_grid_layout/Grids_logical_values_and_writing_modes (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E37

### layout-037 · Emphasis comes from difference, isolation and scale
- **Statement:** The more an element differs from its surroundings in colour, size or shape, the more it stands out. An item set apart from its surroundings demands attention: isolation creates emphasis as proximity creates unity.

### layout-041 · No sideways scrolling on a narrow screen
- **Statement:** A fixed-width page wider than the window is clipped and scrolls sideways on phones and tablets, which is a bad experience: vertical scrolling is acceptable to users, scrolling in all four directions is not. Below a set breakpoint narrow the whole layout to the width of the screen (500 pixels in the authors' example, with a 320-pixel minimum).
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-067 (page 53), GOLDSTEIN-058 (page 286-287, the notes cite 286)
- **Asked of the source:** the narrowest screen width a page should fit today without sideways scrolling (the finding gives 320 pixels).
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate phone E23

### layout-044 · Three key elements as the points of a triangle
- **Statement:** On a sparse page, arrange three key elements as the points of a triangle, ordered for left-to-right reading, so the eye moves from one to the next (in the example: logo, launch date, book cover). Keep the triangle subtle: if it is too strong the eye jumps from point to point and skips the content between.

### layout-045 · Connecting lines carry the eye to key points
- **Statement:** Lines that link headings to text, captions to pictures or navigation buttons to their images speed up reading: the eye travels along them and lands on the composition's key points. A line pulls harder when one end is anchored to a static object such as a label and the other is free, and harder still with a small arrowhead or triangle at the free end.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-098 (page 40), KIRSANOV-269 (page 82)
- **Also in (not read):** KIRSANOV-099, KIRSANOV-270
