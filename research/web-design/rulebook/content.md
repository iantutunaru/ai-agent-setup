### content-001 · Visitors look at the content first
- **Statement:** On a new page people look straight at the content area and its headings and usually ignore the navigation; they turn to it only when the content is not worth their time. They arrive and leave within seconds, so the main content block is the focal point of the design and easy to scan.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-341 (page 251), NIELSEN-136 (page 123), BEAIRD-023 (page 25)
- **Also in (not read):** BEAIRD-015, NIELSEN-133, NIELSEN-135, NIELSEN-592, KIRSANOV-057, BOULTON-179, LUPTON-170, WALTER-067
- **Study (as the book reports it):** NIELSEN-341: Nielsen's own research; the page names no details.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/is-navigation-useful/ (read 2026-11-20)

### content-003 · Visitors are distracted and ready to leave
- **Statement:** Online there is no captive audience: the web is an attention economy where the currency is the user's time, and switching to another site costs almost nothing. Visitors are distracted, juggle tasks, guard their time and leave at once when the content does not serve their goal or they do not see an immediate benefit. Their attention is the scarcest thing a page has, so the design must not waste it and should help them choose what to look at.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** KISSANE-027 (page 16), LUPTON-060 (page 100), LUPTON-057 (page 99), NIELSEN-267 (page 197)
- **Also in (not read):** LUPTON-050
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate content E04, joined by persuasion E85

### content-004 · Content serves the visitor's goal at the moment of need
- **Statement:** Content is right when it helps users reach their goal: what they need, when they need it, in the form that suits them, without wasting their time. Quality alone is not enough; the content has to be relevant to the specific things users want to do on the site.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** KISSANE-001 (page 7), NIELSEN-612 (page 479)

### content-005 · Do not stretch a visit to raise page views
- **Statement:** Do not try to keep visitors on the site as long as possible or make them view many pages: the visitor pays for that time, senses the disregard and moves to sites that help them find what they need quickly. One author stopped using a news site that splits each story into many slow-loading pages, which he guesses was done for page views.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-431 (page 323-324), KRUG-129 (page s98)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate process E08

### content-007 · Publish what customers need, not everything you can
- **Statement:** Too much content makes the needed information harder to find and, with finite resources, lowers the quality of all of it. Publish what customers need. Press releases copied onto the site unedited are a habit, not a service: they matter to a small audience.

### content-009 · Typography serves the meaning the author gives
- **Statement:** Typography exists to honour the content: it clarifies or confirms the meaning of the text and is not used to dress a text up so as to deceive the reader. The meaning, on which the pleasure and ease of reading feed, has to come from the author, not the typographer.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text

### content-010 · Screen reader users hear only the first words
- **Statement:** Screen reader users scan with their ears: they are as impatient as sighted users, often set the speech very fast, and hear only the opening words of each link or line before jumping on, so a keyword placed later may never be heard.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** text, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-169 (page s124)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** Mary Theofanos and Janice Redish watched 16 blind users of screen readers doing tasks on a variety of sites, Interactions, November-December 2003
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/screen-reader-users-on-mobile/ (read 2026-11-20)

### content-011 · Scanners notice words that match their task

### content-012 · Reading from a screen was found slower than reading print
- **Statement:** Reading long text from a screen has been measured slower than reading print, so people avoid long text on screen and web pages need less text than printed pages. The book gives about 25% slower, from unnamed research and the low-resolution monitors of its time.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text

### content-013 · Short, scannable, neutral text measured more usable
- **Statement:** In a test of five versions of the same tourism text, measured usability against the promotional original rose 58% for a version cut by half, 47% for a scannable layout with a bulleted list, 27% for neutral instead of boastful wording, and 124% for all three changes together: the three changes gave the largest gain combined.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** text, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-154 (page 129-131, the notes cite 130-131)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** John Morkes and Jakob Nielsen, usability test of five versions of a Nebraska tourism text; the pages give no number of users
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate content E14

### content-014 · Cut the words by half
- **Statement:** Web copy carries no more than half the words the same material takes in print; cut half the words on a page, then half of what is left. Words nobody reads make a page look daunting, add noise, hide the useful content and push it below the fold.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-047 (page s37), NIELSEN-137 (page 123)

### content-015 · One idea per paragraph, stated in its first sentence
- **Statement:** Scanning users often read only the first sentence of each paragraph, so open each paragraph with its topic sentence and keep it to one idea; a second idea in the same paragraph goes unread when the first does not interest them. Keep sentences simple.

### content-017 · Complex sentences and fine detail are lost on screen
- **Statement:** On screen, long complex sentences full of unfamiliar words have little chance of being read, and fine small letterforms, superscripts, footnotes, endnotes and margin notes tend to disappear: they are hard to see in glare and at low resolution and break the illusion of speed, so links take their place and all parts of the text are set in one size.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text
- **Sources:** BRINGHURST-370 (page 224)

### content-018 · Two or three levels of headings
- **Statement:** Structure an article with two or three levels of headings: the page title plus one or two levels of subheadings. Most pages have no real hierarchy beyond the main heading, and needing more than two or three levels is an argument for the academic page style.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text, check

### content-019 · A heading says plainly what follows and stands alone
- **Statement:** A heading or headline says plainly what the page or section is about, so a reader knows without reading the body. Headlines are often seen out of context (article lists, search results, bookmarks) and users often read only the headlines in a list, so each must stand alone; clever wording and wordplay are misunderstood.

### content-021 · Put the most informative word first
- **Statement:** Start a page title or headline with its most informative word, the one most likely to match the visitor's need (organisation name, author or subject), for scanning and alphabetical lists. ### content-022 · A headline tells readers what is in it for them

### content-023 · In the title, the page first and the site name last
- **Statement:** Put in the title the page's topic, the section it belongs to and sometimes the site name, ordered from the specific to the general: the current page first, the site name last. Google accepts the site name at either the start or the end, set off by a hyphen, colon or pipe, and cuts a long title to fit the device width; the specific-first order is the book's practice. The book adds that search engines weigh title text above page text; Google's guidance does not say so.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text, check
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-324 (page 97)
- **Asked of the source:** how search engines weigh and display the page title today, and the order they recommend.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate content E30

### content-029 · Tell search engines and programs what the page holds
- **Statement:** Plain markup does not tell a search engine whether a page is a recipe or a film review. Name the object on the page (a person, event, product) and its properties as structured data, and give exact dates and times in a machine-readable form, so that search engines give better results and an event can be lifted into the user's calendar. Google reads structured data in JSON-LD (its recommendation), Microdata or RDFa and uses it to understand the page and to show rich results; the time element carries a date in machine-readable form.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** check
- **Sources:** ROBBINS-055 (page 142), ROBBINS-049 (page 132)
- **Also in (not read):** ROBBINS-056
- **Asked of the source:** which structured data formats and vocabularies search engines read today, and what they do with them.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/intro-structured-data (read 2026-11-20)
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/time (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate content E36

### content-034 · Do not bury the lead
- **Statement:** Writers who know a subject well get lost in its details and let the main point slip down the text; write the opening sentence first and spend most of the effort on it. The lead is what the facts mean to the reader, not a condensed list of who, what and when: for a notice of a staff conference, the lead is that there is no school next Thursday.

### content-036 · Split by topic and move the detail to linked pages
- **Statement:** Keep each page short without losing depth: split the material into logical pieces, each on one topic, and move long details and material of interest to few readers onto secondary pages reached by links. - **Use:** look, text

### content-037 · A text read in sequence stays on one page
- **Statement:** Do not chop one long linear text into numbered pages: for a sequential text, extra links and loading extra pages only make reading harder. If a text must keep its linear flow, leave it on one page; scrolling is a nuisance, but opening new pages in the middle of reading is worse. On a phone, a story split into many small pages that each load slowly and repeat the same photo to scroll past made Krug pick another source whenever a story led to that site: in the news story shown, the first screen of each page holds the masthead, a row of five icon tabs, a search field, a section label, the headline, the date and the photo, with not one line of story text, and after scrolling a page gives two paragraphs and a "1 / 8" counter with a Next button.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-180 (page 151), KRUG-130 (page s98), KRUG-222 (page s98)
- **Needs source:** that current guidance still advises against spreading one article over several pages on a phone

### content-038 · One topic per page, each topic on one page
- **Statement:** Split content into pages logically, one topic per page and each topic on one page only; a page focused on one clear topic is the one others link to, while a page mixing topics rarely gets linked and confuses people who arrive by a link. Too many tiny pages are also bad, since each click costs effort; the book cites a "two-click rule" that no page is more than two clicks from the home page.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check

### content-042 · A person's name links to a profile page
- **Statement:** A person's name used as a link leads to their profile page: users expect a link to bring information about what it names, and being thrown into an email program by an ordinary-looking link on a person's name is jarring. The profile page holds a small ordinary photo (people often know a face but not the name), a short note on their experience, interests and skills, a link to the list of their articles where they write for the site, and the contact details they want public, ideally an email link. On an organisation's intranet, a staff page also links to their department and their projects.

### content-046 · Where the statement of what the site is goes
- **Statement:** Users look for the statement of what the site is in three places: a tagline tied visually to the site's name, a short welcome blurb in a prominent block at the top left or centre of the content, and a "learn more" item, in the book often a short explanatory video. Do not use a mission statement as the welcome blurb. The book's examples of a blurb: a heading naming the product category over one sentence saying what the service does and for whom (Rezdy), and one factual sentence with numbers (Idealab). Nielsen Norman Group finds no standard behaviour with video: some visitors watch it, some never do, so whatever a video says must also be on the page as text.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** KRUG-216 (page s65), KRUG-085 (page s65)
- **Asked of the source:** whether visitors still expect and watch a short explanatory video on a home page.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/video-usability/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate content E57

### content-047 · A tagline states the value, a motto does not
- **Statement:** A tagline sits beside, above or below the site's name, says clearly what the organisation does in about six to eight words, shows a benefit and a difference no one else could claim, and is clever only when that helps the benefit. A motto (a lofty ideal such as "You're in good hands") tells a stranger nothing about what the site is. Even well-known brands benefit from a tagline, since the purpose online is never exactly the same as offline.
- **Kind:** practice
