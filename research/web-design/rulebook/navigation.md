### navigation-001 · Build the structure on users' tasks, not the organisation chart
- **Statement:** The site's structure follows the tasks users want to do and how they see the information, not the company's departments, even if one page must combine two departments' information. In usability tests of an e-commerce home page draft, navigation built on the users' model reached 80% task success against 9% for the one built on the company's internal view.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-337 (page 247), NIELSEN-010 (page 24), NIELSEN-335 (page 245), NIELSEN-334 (page 245)
- **Also in (not read):** KISSANE-008, NIELSEN-325, BEAIRD-011
- **Study (as the book reports it):** NIELSEN-337: an e-commerce project of the author's, usability tests of a draft home page with two navigation schemes, 80% success with the scheme built on the users' model against 9% with the one built on the company's internal view
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E01

### navigation-002 · Getting people to the right page is harder than making one page clear
- **Statement:** Starting from the home page, only 42% of users in one study reached the page that held the answer to a simple question. The site's architecture is therefore harder and more important to design than single pages, and no navigation design rescues a chaotic structure.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-270 (page 203), NIELSEN-269 (page 202), NIELSEN-324 (page 239)
- **Also in (not read):** NIELSEN-316
- **Study (as the book reports it):** NIELSEN-270: Jared Spool and colleagues, users given a simple task starting on the home page; 42% reached the right page in time
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/failure-of-corporate-websites/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E02

### navigation-003 · Cut the navigation to a minimum on form pages
- **Statement:** On form pages (checkout, registration, subscription, feedback, preferences) reduce the persistent navigation to the site ID, a link Home and any utilities that help fill in the form, so users are not distracted from finishing.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-058 (page s49)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate forms E09

### navigation-004 · Navigation has to give the sense of place the web lacks
- **Statement:** Navigation shows the site's hierarchy so users can get somewhere and know where they are; it also shows what the site contains and how to use it (where to begin, what the options are), so it should be all the instructions needed. People who cannot find their way around a site leave and do not come back.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-054 (page s45), KRUG-055 (page s46), KRUG-050 (page s42)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E03

### navigation-005 · The home page shows where to start
- **Statement:** After a quick look at the home page a visitor should know where to start to search, to browse and to sample the best content, and where to sign in or begin a step-by-step process. A home page offers a directory of the main content sections, a short summary of key news or offers, and search; entry points look like what they are and are labelled plainly ("Search", "Browse by Category", "Sign in", "Start here").
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check, text
- **Sources:** KRUG-091 (page s68), NIELSEN-283 (page 209)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate layout E18

### navigation-006 · Group like things so visitors can skip what they do not want
- **Statement:** Put like information together in logical groups: product categories exist chiefly so visitors can skip the products they do not care about. A site that does not help people narrow down to a group gets abandoned, and a visitor who cannot find something in a logical place finds the site unpleasant.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-158 (page 135), BEAIRD-079 (page 62)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E04

### navigation-007 · Sort the sections into a site map before drawing pages
- **Statement:** Sort the sections and pages before drawing, for example with sticky notes arranged on a wall in menu order or a mind map with the home page in the centre and one branch per main section. Balance too many choices at once against information buried too many clicks from the home page. In the example most sections hold three to five pages, only the shop goes one level deeper, and the map's main branches reappear as the menu bar of the home page wireframe.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-273 (page 62-63, the notes cite 62), BEAIRD-012 (page 20)
- **Also in (not read):** KISSANE-078
- **Conflict:** KRUG-041 (in navigation-008) says the number of clicks is not what counts, only how hard each click is.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E05

### navigation-008 · Judge a path by the thought each click takes, not by the number of clicks
- **Statement:** Users accept many clicks if each is an easy, unambiguous choice and they stay confident they are on the right track; as a rule of thumb, three mindless clicks equal one click that needs thought. Fewer clicks matter more when users repeat the same path often or when pages load slowly.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-041 (page s35), KRUG-043 (page s35)
- **Conflict:** BEAIRD-012 (in navigation-007) treats information many clicks from the home page as a fault to balance against.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E06

### navigation-009 · Use a tree for varied material and a chain for a true sequence
- **Statement:** A tree (hierarchy) suits varied or complex material such as catalogues and collections of articles; a linear chain suits material that is an ordered sequence, such as the steps of an order, where entering midway makes little sense. Most sites hang chains off the branches of a tree. A third form is tabular: pages classified on several attributes, so users can browse along any of them.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-293 (page 89-90, the notes cite 89), NIELSEN-333 (page 245)
- **Also in (not read):** KIRSANOV-294, NIELSEN-356
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E07

### navigation-010 · In a chain of pages give previous, next and a numbered link to every page
- **Statement:** A linear section needs at least "previous" and "next" links. Numbered links to every page in the chain, with the current one marked, are much more convenient, because they allow a jump from any page to any other, as on search results pages.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-521 (page 160), KIRSANOV-338 (page 100)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E08

### navigation-011 · Set an unfamiliar kind of navigation apart and keep it simple
- **Statement:** A navigation type the visitor has not met elsewhere on the site, such as a linear chain inside a hierarchical site, is kept simple and visibly set apart, for example in a different, brighter colour, so it reads as a new element.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-522 (page 160)

### navigation-012 · Label choices by what they hold, never by number
- **Statement:** Menus of numbers (page numbers copied from a print edition, numbered options like TV channels) make users guess what a number stands for and cannot be remembered for a return visit; menus of article titles or sections ("Food" rather than "page 14") let them choose. A print publication's linear structure is not copied onto the web, where everything is one click away.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-303 (page 222), NIELSEN-330 (page 243)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E10

### navigation-013 · Keep the top-level bar to ten items at most, better seven
- **Statement:** The top-level navigation bar has no more than ten buttons, better seven; the author gives this as the most same-level items people handle without effort, citing psychologists' research without naming a study. With more than ten top sections, merge some or show two levels on the bar with sections and subsections styled differently.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-339 (page 100-101, the notes cite 100)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E11

### navigation-014 · Do not make users classify themselves at the top level
- **Statement:** Avoid top-level choices that ask users to say what they are when the categories overlap or are unclear ("Home" or "Office" for someone buying for a home office, subscriber or member): users hesitate, doubt their choice and may give up.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-044 (page s35)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E12

### navigation-015 · Keep navigation simple; add signposts only as the site's size demands
- **Statement:** Keep the system as simple as possible, and where it has to be complex give people more signs to find their way. Complex combined navigation (breadth at the top and bottom levels plus partial depth) suits only very large sites with very varied content, over 10,000 pages; smaller sites, and large sites with uniform content, are easier to use with simple navigation.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-343 (page 252), THALER-184 (page 158)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E13

### navigation-016 · Visitors find the site harder to navigate than its makers do
- **Statement:** Programmers and graphic designers have far better spatial ability than average, and young designers remember cryptic codes such as URLs more easily than older people. Assume most visitors find it much harder to navigate the site than the people who built it.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-511 (page 390)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E14

### navigation-017 · A subsite keeps its ties to the parent site
- **Statement:** A subsite (pages with a shared style and navigation for one class of users or one kind of use inside a large site) has its own main page, links on every page to that page and to the site's home page, and the global options such as site-wide search. It does not try to become independent of its parent.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-362 (page 270-271)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E15

### navigation-018 · People take the first reasonable option, not the best one
- **Statement:** People pick the first workable option instead of comparing options (observed in fire commanders at fire scenes). On a page users click as soon as a link seems likely to lead to their goal, because a wrong guess costs only a click on Back.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, text
- **Sources:** KRUG-017 (page s25)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** KRUG-017: Gary Klein's team, a study of field commanders at fire scenes, who compared no options and took the first reasonable plan
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/satisficing/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E17

### navigation-019 · Back must always work
- **Statement:** Back is among the most used navigation features: in early studies of web behaviour it came second, after clicking a link, and users still rely on it, on phones through the back gesture or button. It makes a wrong guess cheap and lets users explore knowing they can return to firm ground. Nothing on the site may break it.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-120 (page 110), KRUG-018 (page s25)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** NIELSEN-120: the earliest studies of user behaviour on the web, named with no details: Back is the second most used navigation feature in browsers, after following a link
- **Asked of the source:** how much Back is used today, on phones with back gestures as well as on desktop browsers.
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-control-and-freedom/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E18

### navigation-020 · A link that names its target keeps users going
- **Statement:** Links that clearly name their target give off a strong "scent of information" that keeps users going; ambiguous or poorly worded links do not.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** text, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-042 (page s35)
- **Study (as the book reports it):** KRUG-042: Peter Pirolli and Stuart Card, information foraging research, Xerox PARC

### navigation-022 · Follow the conventions users know from other sites
- **Statement:** Users spend a few minutes on a site, experience the web as a whole, do not read site-specific help and expect each site to work as the others do; they complain about navigation that differs from what most sites use. An element in wide use, such as the shopping cart, is understood without learning, which also obliges designers to use it where it does not quite fit. Replace a convention only with something so clear that it needs no learning, or so valuable that a small learning curve is worth it; Krug's classic failure: custom-made scrollbars.
- **Kind:** rule
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-310 (page 231), NIELSEN-351 (page 262), NIELSEN-307 (page 230), KRUG-023 (page s29), NIELSEN-352 (page 263)
- **Also in (not read):** BOULTON-158
- **Study (as the book reports it):** NIELSEN-310, NIELSEN-351: the author's own research, users complaining about or displeased with sites whose navigation works differently from most; no details or numbers given
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/videos/jakobs-law-internet-ux/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E22, joined by process E13

### navigation-023 · Back an inventive navigation with a conventional one
- **Statement:** An inventive navigation, such as a photo used as a menu, is backed by a conventional category navigation as well, because the novelty wears off. In the example a small "browse" menu stays in the same place on every screen and a breadcrumb trail shows the path.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-024 (page s29), KRUG-198 (page s29)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, image, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E23

### navigation-024 · Avoid building a site on a metaphor or a real-world place
- **Statement:** Avoid building a site on a metaphor in most cases: it tempts designers into cleverness that fits the metaphor and ignores users' goals, it is often too narrow to cover everything the site does and forces odd solutions, and it is usually better to say literally what each element is for. A metaphor helps when it gives the design a unifying frame and lets users apply what they already know, as the shopping cart does. Geographic metaphors (the floors of a virtual business centre, a virtual fairground, virtual shopping malls) place sections arbitrarily, so users cannot guess what is where, and add needless steps where the web could jump straight to the destination; a site redesigned from an airport-counter metaphor to a literal design became much easier to use. Exception: a site that is itself about geography.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-304 (page 224), NIELSEN-305 (page 226-227, the notes cite 226), NIELSEN-263 (page 195), NIELSEN-300 (page 222), NIELSEN-301 (page 222)
- **Conflict:** KIRSANOV-348 (in navigation-025) presents the navigation bar as the usual carrier of the site's theme or metaphor.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E24, joined by content E77

### navigation-025 · The navigation bar often carries the site's theme
- **Statement:** As one of the most graphic areas of the page, the navigation bar often carries the site's main theme or metaphor: tabs, road signs, branches or leaves, diagram elements, book pages or spines, stairs.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** KIRSANOV-348 (page 103)
- **Conflict:** NIELSEN-304, NIELSEN-305 (in navigation-024) warn against metaphors in navigation as too narrow and arbitrary.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E25

### navigation-026 · Avoid three-dimensional navigation
- **Statement:** Abstract information spaces are not physical: moving in three dimensions is not natural, screen and input are two-dimensional so control is clumsy, and much of the information is hidden while moving. 3D navigation of a website usually confuses users and impresses only in demos where the presenter knows where everything is.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-360 (page 269), NIELSEN-262 (page 194-195, the notes cite 194)
- **Needs source:** whether any current evidence shows a 3D interface faster than a plain 2D one for typical tasks; the book says none had been shown.
- **Current source:** none found (searched 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E26

### navigation-027 · Keep the global navigation in the same place with the same look on every page
- **Statement:** Persistent navigation sits in the same place and looks the same on every page: it confirms users are still on the same site and they learn how it works only once. A page may get its own version of the bar where logic and ease of use call for it, but the version stays close to the standard order of the buttons.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-057 (page s48), KIRSANOV-353 (page 103)

### navigation-029 · A piece best read whole may hold the site navigation back to its end
- **Statement:** Web readers are goal-driven and jump between pages. Where a piece is best read whole, like a magazine article, the page can drop the site navigation until the end so readers are not pulled away.

### navigation-030 · Keep the utilities apart from the sections and less prominent
- **Statement:** Split a large bar in two: content (the topical sections people come for) and tools or utilities (sign in, help, site map, search, cart, about, contact), which sit outside the content hierarchy and are slightly less prominent. The persistent navigation holds only the four or five most used utilities; the rest go in the footer. Secondary navigation, search and outgoing links are easy to find but do not dominate the content.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-061 (page s52), KIRSANOV-340 (page 101), BEAIRD-017 (page 22)
- **Conflict:** KRUG-208, KRUG-207 (in layout-025) put the sections in a horizontal bar under the site ID and the utilities at the top right, where KIRSANOV-340 puts content links in a left column.
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E31

### navigation-031 · Put the main navigation near the top, every main item above the fold
- **Statement:** Users expect navigation at the top of the page. Whether the menu runs across or down the side, place it as near the top as possible and keep every main item above the fold; links belong above the fold above all else, since clicking a link costs less effort than scrolling.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** BEAIRD-021 (page 25), KIRSANOV-314 (page 94)
- **Conflict:** NIELSEN-177 (in navigation-082) says to avoid links that jump within the same page, which KIRSANOV-314 also suggests for long pages (a contents list of the page's sections at the top, a link back to the top beside each section heading).
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E32

### navigation-036 · A few chosen outbound links, each with a note
- **Statement:** Choose outbound links selectively: a few very good pages beat a list of every similar site, because users usually have time for only about 10% of the links listed; for those who want everything, link to one comprehensive directory. Add a short annotation to each link so users can judge at once which are worth following.

### navigation-038 · Put the site's logo on every page, in a fixed place, linked to the home page
- **Statement:** Users view no more than four to five pages of a site per visit, arrive on pages by jumping, and do not know which site they are on unless told. Put the logo or other site identifier on every page in a fixed place, at the top and preferably at or near the top left for left-to-right languages (top right for right-to-left), and link it to the home page, so the home page is one click from every inner page. The identifier stands for the whole site, the top of its hierarchy, and builds brand recognition.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-311 (page 231), NIELSEN-297 (page 220), BEAIRD-020 (page 24), KRUG-059 (page s50)
- **Also in (not read):** NIELSEN-448, KIRSANOV-341, NIELSEN-457
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E39, joined by trust E64, joined by layout E09

### navigation-039 · Keep a way to the home page in sight on every page
- **Statement:** On the web people have no sense of location; the home page is a fixed point, and a way back to it is a reset for a fresh start. Keep it in sight on every page: nearly all users expect the site ID to link Home, but not all realise the logo is a link, so also give an explicit "Home" link or button on every page, listed with the main sections.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** KRUG-053 (page s45), KRUG-062 (page s53), NIELSEN-458 (page 341), NIELSEN-298 (page 220)
- **Asked of the source:** whether users today still fail to see the logo as a link to the home page; one book calls the convention new and asks for the Home link "for the next few years"
- **Current source:** confirmed by https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-links/ (read 2026-11-20)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, passage, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E40, joined by navigation E41

### navigation-042 · Every page tells a visitor who lands on it which site this is
- **Statement:** People arrive on inner pages from search engines, email and links without passing the home page, so every inner page carries the site name and orients them about who you are. How much identity it needs depends on how often people enter deep and how well known the site is: a well-known site needs only a link home, a lesser-known one more identifying information and a larger name and logo. Inner pages still focus on their own information.
- **Kind:** practice
- **Use:** look, check
- **Sources:** NIELSEN-295 (page 219), NIELSEN-296 (page 220), KRUG-084 (page s64)
- **Read:** 2026-11-20, page, Opus agent, each part checked by Claude; candidate navigation E42

### navigation-043 · Visitors can arrive at any inner page
- **Statement:** Anyone may link to any page of a site without asking, including inner pages that skip the front door; the owner finds out who links in only from the server's referrer statistics or by searching.

### navigation-099 · Keep the standard link colours

### navigation-140 · Do not open a new window or tab the user did not ask for
- **Statement:** New browser windows or tabs clutter the screen and break the Back button, since a new one does not inherit the history; people often do not notice that one has opened, above all on phones, where the old window is never visible, and wonder why Back does not work. The one good reason to open one is content the user must refer to while finishing a task in the other.
